## Architecture proposal: Fathom clone (24-hour build)

The main idea: **a 1-hour, 8-person call fits in a single AI request** (about 12–15k tokens, and the model's limit is 1M). So we don't split long transcripts because we have to. We split them to finish the summary within seconds of the call ending, to tie every action item to the exact moment it was said, and to process the meeting while it's still running.

### 1. Stack

| Layer | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| App | **Next.js 15 (App Router) + TypeScript** | Pages and backend in one codebase, one deploy, streaming responses built in. |
| Database | **Postgres on Neon** | Survives redeploys, and gives us full-text search (plus pgvector for semantic search) with no separate search service. Free tier, one-click Vercel setup. |
| Data access | **Drizzle ORM** + SQL migrations | Typed, lightweight, and lets us write raw SQL where it matters (search, time-range queries). |
| UI | Tailwind + shadcn/ui, TanStack Virtual | Fast to build a polished UI; the virtual list keeps a 1,500-line transcript smooth. |
| AI | **Anthropic TypeScript SDK, `claude-opus-5`** | Structured JSON output, prompt caching, streaming. |
| Hosting | **Vercel + Neon** | Deploy on push, a copy of the database per preview, and code that runs after the response is sent (useful for background AI jobs). |

I'm leaving out Redis, separate job-queue services and file storage. They're more moving parts than 24 hours can support, and nothing below needs them.

### 2. Data model
```
users ─< calendar_events >─ meetings ─< meeting_participants (speaker_idx, color, talk_ms)
                              │
                              ├─< transcript_segments  (seq, speaker_id, start_ms, end_ms, text, tsv)
                              ├─< highlights           (at_ms, label, created_by)
                              ├─< chunk_notes          (chunk_idx, window_ms, notes jsonb, prompt_ver)
                              ├─  meeting_knowledge    (canonical facts jsonb, transcript_hash)
                              ├─< summaries            (template_id, content jsonb, model, prompt_ver, status)
                              ├─< action_items         (text, owner_id, due, segment_ids[], status)
                              └─< clips                (slug, start_ms, end_ms, title, views)
templates (id, name, instructions, output_schema)
```
Database choices that affect performance:
- **One row per transcript segment,** indexed on `(meeting_id, start_ms)`. A clip, highlight or "jump to moment" is a quick indexed range lookup (`start_ms < $end AND end_ms > $start`), never a scan of the whole transcript.
- **The search index is computed by the database itself,** weighted so a speaker's name ranks above ordinary words, with highlighted snippets. Search works across meetings with no extra service.
- **Stats are calculated once when a meeting ends:** duration, talk time per speaker, action-item counts. The dashboard reads plain columns instead of recalculating on every page load.
- **Summaries are cached per meeting, template and prompt version,** with a unique key. Switching templates reads the cache or fills it once, and a prompt change can't serve an out-of-date summary.
- **Live transcript lines are saved in batches** (about every 2 seconds), not one database write per line.

### 3. AI pipeline: extract once, render per template

**Step 0 – Shrink the transcript.** Merge consecutive lines from the same speaker into one short line per turn, like `[#0412 23:14 S3] text`, with a list of speaker names at the top. This roughly halves the tokens, and the `#id` gives the model a way to point back to exact lines.

**Step 1 – Notes on each section, in parallel and during the call.** Split the call into windows of about 10 minutes, cutting only at speaker changes and overlapping slightly. Each window produces structured notes (checked against a Zod schema) listing topics, decisions, candidate action items with a quoted piece of evidence and line IDs, open questions and notable moments.
- **New idea: the notes for a window are generated when the live playback clock passes the end of that window.** By the time the call ends, most of this work is already done.

**Step 2 – Merge into one meeting record.** Combine the window notes and remove duplicates into one canonical record: action items, decisions, topics with time ranges, and what each speaker contributed. **Our server then checks every cited line ID and quote against the stored transcript.** A missing ID or a quote that doesn't match gets dropped or flagged, so action items the model invented don't reach the UI.

**Step 3 – Write the summary in the chosen template.** The request is ordered so the unchanging parts are cached:
```
system (role, rules, output contract)  →  [cache] transcript + meeting record  →  template instructions + output schema
```
The transcript part of the prompt is identical for every template, so switching templates re-reads it from cache and only pays for the short, template-specific part, and the result streams into the UI. The default template is generated in advance, the others when first requested.

**Rules in the action-item prompt:**
- Only real commitments count ("I'll send it Friday" counts, "we should maybe…" doesn't).
- The owner must be a participant or `unassigned`.
- A due date appears only if one was actually said.
- Each item needs a quoted piece of evidence and line IDs.
- Hypothetical and rejected ideas are left out.

**Model settings:**
- `claude-opus-5` with adaptive thinking, and server-side fallback enabled so a refused request retries on another model automatically.
- Low effort for the per-window notes, higher for the merge step.
- A cheaper model for the per-window notes is an option, but that's your decision.

**Ask/search:** full-text search finds the best-matching lines across all meetings, then Claude answers using only those lines and cites them. Clicking a citation jumps to that moment.

**Seed data without an API key:** all AI outputs for the seeded meetings are generated once and committed as JSON. The app starts complete with no API key, and a live key only matters for new recordings and template switches.

### 4. Recording: a scripted playback engine
- **Scripted playback, no media files.** A recording is a timed transcript plus a virtual clock. The "video" is a grid of 8 speaker tiles drawn in the browser: the active speaker glows, a waveform moves, captions show, and there's a timer. There are no media files to store and nothing breaks on deploy.
- **Speed control (1×, 4×, 16×, 60×)** lets a demo play the 60-minute call in a minute while still producing the full volume of data.
- **Optional voices:** the browser's built-in speech synthesis can read each speaker with a different voice, so we get audio with no audio files.
- **Clips replay the same engine for their time range,** so a shared clip link plays like a video with captions.
- **Stretch goal: a real microphone mode.** It would record the mic and transcribe it in the browser (Chrome only) into the same pipeline. It's optional, but it would show the pipeline accepts real input.

### 5. State management
- **Server-rendered pages for reads** (no client-side loading chains), and **Server Actions for changes**.
- **The URL holds state that should be shareable:** `?t=1834000&template=sales&q=pricing`, so a link to a moment or view is just the URL.
- **One small store for the playback clock** (Zustand). Components subscribe only to the values they need, so the 1,500-line transcript doesn't re-render on every tick.
- **Finding the current line** uses a binary search over the start times.
- **Highlights and action-item toggles update instantly** and save in the background.

About 9,000 words and 1,200–1,500 lines. That's roughly 5 parallel window-note requests, 1 merge, and 1 request per template (mostly read from cache). The database cost is one indexed range per view, and search is served from the database's index. It fits comfortably on free tiers.

## 3. Three bottlenecks and how we design around them

**1. Vercel time limits vs. an hour-long call and a multi-step AI pipeline**
*Risk:* a server function can't run a 60-minute simulation, and a single request that summarizes everything in one go is slow and can hit time limits.
*Design:*
- **The browser runs the clock, not the server.** The playback engine sends lines to `/ingest` in small batches.
- **The AI work is split into short, separate jobs.** When the clock passes a ~10-minute window, a job for that window starts, finishes in seconds, and saves its result.
- **Each job is safe to re-run.** The status lives in the database, so a retry never produces duplicates.
- By the time the call ends, most notes are already done, and only the merge and the default summary remain.
- A 60× speed setting lets a demo play the full hour in about a minute.

**2. Reliability of AI output: invented action items, slow or costly template switches**
*Risk:* the model can invent owners or tasks, return malformed JSON, or make every template switch a slow full re-summarization.
*Design:*
- **Every output is checked against a Zod schema,** so the UI never has to parse free-form text.
- **Every action item must include line IDs and a quoted piece of evidence.** Our server checks both against the stored transcript and drops or flags anything that doesn't match.
- **Template prompts are ordered for caching:** fixed rules, then the transcript (cached), then template instructions. A switch pays only for the short, template-specific part and streams in.
- **Results are cached** per meeting, template and prompt version.
- **Server-side fallback** covers refused requests, and the pre-generated seed outputs mean the demo still works if the AI API is down or slow.

**3. Seed data realism and volume (the "no empty dashboards" rule)**

  - Meet sends the few loudest speakers as separate streams, so the bot can match streams to people using stream IDs and the active-speaker indicators in the page.
  - Google's official Meet Media API (developer preview, enrollment required) aims to replace this.
- **Microsoft Teams:** a Microsoft Graph calling bot using "application-hosted media" receives separate audio per participant with participant IDs. It historically required Windows and .NET.

**Separating speakers, from best to worst:**
1. Per-participant streams from the platform: names come from the roster and no diarization is needed.
2. A mixed stream plus the platform's active-speaker events, aligned on timestamps.
3. Mixed audio plus diarization (what we have): the least accurate.

Each speaker's stream then goes to streaming speech recognition, gets timestamped on a shared meeting clock, and is merged into one transcript. Meanwhile the recording is combined into one file and uploaded to the media pipeline.

**What running this costs operationally:**
- Scraping bots break whenever Meet changes its page layout.
- Bots get stuck in lobbies or blocked by host-only recording policies, and must detect when a meeting has ended.
- Platforms push back on automation.
- Compute bills grow with the number of simultaneous meetings, not users.
- That's why buying the bot layer (e.g. Recall.ai, paid) is a common choice even for well-funded teams.

## 1. Plugin and event architecture

**Build it in two tiers:**

| Tier | Mechanism | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|
| **Out-of-process** (primary) | Signed webhooks, a scoped REST/ingest API, the SSE stream | Anyone, in any language; safe in a multi-tenant hosted version |
| **In-process adapters** | npm packages registered in config, implementing a small set of interfaces: `CalendarSource`, `CaptureSource`, `Transcriber`, `LlmProvider`, `MediaStore`, `Sink` | Self-hosters only, as trusted code. Never run third-party code inside a shared host |

**Outbound events** (what users react to):
- Write each event to an outbox table in the same database transaction as the state change, so an event is never lost or sent for a change that rolled back.
- A dispatcher delivers them:
  - signed per the Standard Webhooks spec;
  - at least once, with retries, backoff and a dead-letter table;
  - each event carries an ID so receivers can ignore duplicates.
- This fits serverless: `after()` sends immediately and a Vercel Cron job sweeps anything missed. It's the same pattern as our `processing_jobs` queue.
- Event catalog:
  - lifecycle events: `meeting.started`, `meeting.ended`, `notes.ready`, `action_item.created`, `clip.created`;
  - **transcript lines don't get one webhook each** (an 8-person hour is about 1,000 lines). Send `transcript.lines_appended` with a seq range, and point real-time consumers at the SSE stream we already have.

**Inbound APIs are the real extensibility, and this is what removes central OAuth:**

## 1. Why no Google Calendar sign-in (we used an iCal link instead)
- **Google has to review the app first.** Reading someone's calendar needs Google permissions it classes as "sensitive". To use them publicly, Google reviews the app: a verified domain, a privacy policy, a demo video and a manual review. That takes **days to weeks**, not 24 hours.
- **Until Google approves it, users see warnings.** Everyone gets a "Google hasn't verified this app" screen, and only up to 100 invited test users can sign in at all.
- **Too much extra plumbing for a day.** You'd have to store and refresh Google tokens, and live calendar updates need push notifications to a verified https domain. It's a lot of moving parts for the time we had.
- **What we did instead:**
  - Every Google or Outlook calendar has a **secret iCal address**. You paste it once and Parley reads your events, attendees, agendas and meeting links.
  - No Google review is needed and it works today.
  - The tradeoff is that it updates on a schedule, not instantly.
- **Why sign-in still works:** Google sign-in only asks for name, email and photo, which need **no review**. That's why sign-in works and calendar access didn't.

## 2. Why no bot that joins Meet, Zoom or Teams
- **Google Meet:**
  - There's no normal public way for a bot to join a call and hear it.
  - Google's live-audio access for Meet is a **developer preview**. You have to enroll, and even the meeting's participants have to be in the program.
  - Google's regular Meet API only gives you recordings and transcripts **after** the call. It needs a paid Google Workspace account with recording switched on.
- **Zoom:**
  - The bot is a full Zoom client running on a server.
  - Getting raw audio needs the host's recording permission, and publishing the app needs **Zoom's review**.
- **Microsoft Teams:** bots that handle live audio run on **Windows servers only**, through Azure.
- **The main blocker, our hosting:**
  - A bot has to stay inside the call for the whole hour.
  - Vercel runs short server tasks with time limits and no always-on processes. A bot can't live there.
  - It would need its own always-on servers.
- **The paid alternative:** services like Recall.ai handle all of this, but they **charge per hour of meeting**. That's why we skipped the bot.
- **What we did instead:**
  - Parley records **from your browser**: your mic plus the meeting tab's audio.
  - It works on any call you can open in a tab.
  - No bot appears in the call, and nothing needs approval from Zoom or Google.

## 3. Why we didn't record video or use a video model
- **Storage:**
  - One hour of meeting audio is about **15–20 MB**. One hour of 720p video is about **600–700 MB**.
  - Our free Neon database has **0.5 GB**. Audio fits about 30 hours; a single video call would nearly fill it.
  - Video needs separate file storage plus a paid video service like Mux.
- **What makes the notes is the words.** Summaries, decisions and action items come from what people **say**, and the transcript has nearly all of it. Video adds faces and slides, which rarely change the notes.
- **Cost and speed:**
  - Video AI models look at frames. One frame every few seconds is **thousands of images per call**.
  - Each image costs far more than text and is slower to process.
  - Live notes during the call only work because text is fast and cheap.
- **Model choice:**
  - Our setup is Deepgram speech-to-text, then **any text model**: Claude, ChatGPT or free Hugging Face models.
  - Most free and cheap models are text-only. Bring-your-own-keys only works if any model can do the job, and a video pipeline would lock us into expensive vision models.
- **Devices:**
  - Recording tab video in the browser is heavy on CPU and upload.
  - Phone browsers can't capture a tab at all. Audio is what works on every device.
- **Privacy:** video means storing people's faces, which is more sensitive data and a bigger consent question than audio and text.
- **In one line for the video:** "Notes come from words, so we chose a speech-to-text pipeline. It's roughly 40× cheaper to store, fast enough for live notes, and works with any AI model."
